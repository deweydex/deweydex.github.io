\begin{quote}
\textbf{What This Worksheet Is About}

Quadratics aren't just abstract algebra --- they show up everywhere: -
Objects flying through the air follow parabolic paths - Trigonometric
functions can be approximated by polynomials - Probability distributions
use binomial expansions - Compound growth involves expanding expressions
like \((1 + r)^n\)

This worksheet connects the algebra you've learned to real applications.
\end{quote}

\begin{center}\rule{0.5\linewidth}{0.5pt}\end{center}

\hypertarget{part-a-projectile-motion-12-problems}{%
\subsection{Part A: Projectile Motion (12
problems)}\label{part-a-projectile-motion-12-problems}}

When you throw a ball, drop an object, or launch a rocket, its height
follows a quadratic equation:

\[h(t) = h_0 + v_0 t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2\]

Where: - \(h(t)\) = height at time \(t\) - \(h_0\) = initial height
(starting height) - \(v_0\) = initial velocity (positive = upward,
negative = downward) - \(g\) = acceleration due to gravity
(\(\approx 10 \text{ m/s}^2\) or \(32 \text{ ft/s}^2\)) - \(t\) = time

\textbf{Simplified form (using \(g = 10\) m/s²):}

\[h(t) = h_0 + v_0 t - 5t^2\]

\hypertarget{part-c-polynomial-approximations-of-trig-functions-10-problems}{%
\subsection{Part C: Polynomial Approximations of Trig Functions (10
problems)}\label{part-c-polynomial-approximations-of-trig-functions-10-problems}}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Key Insight:} Sine, cosine, and other transcendental functions
can be approximated by polynomials! These are called \textbf{Taylor
series} approximations.

For small values of \(\theta\) (in radians):
\end{quote}

\[\sin(\theta) \approx \theta - \frac{\theta^3}{6} + \frac{\theta^5}{120} - \cdots\]

\[\cos(\theta) \approx 1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2} + \frac{\theta^4}{24} - \cdots\]

\textbf{These are just polynomials!} All your algebra skills apply.

\begin{center}\rule{0.5\linewidth}{0.5pt}\end{center}

\textbf{30.} \textbf{Connection to Euler's Formula}

The famous formula \(e^{i\theta} = \cos(\theta) + i\sin(\theta)\)
connects exponentials and trig.

Using the polynomial approximations, verify this is consistent for small
\(\theta\) by comparing: -
\(e^{i\theta} \approx 1 + i\theta + \frac{(i\theta)^2}{2} = 1 + i\theta - \frac{\theta^2}{2}\)
(since \(i^2 = -1\)) -
\(\cos(\theta) + i\sin(\theta) \approx \left(1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2}\right) + i(\theta)\)

Are they the same?

\subsection{Part D: Binomial Expansion and Probability (10
problems)}\label{part-d-binomial-expansion-and-probability-10-problems}}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Connection:} When you flip a coin \(n\) times, the probability
of getting exactly \(k\) heads involves the binomial coefficient from
Pascal's Triangle!
\end{quote}

\[P(k \text{ heads in } n \text{ flips}) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}\]

Where \(\binom{n}{k}\) is the entry in row \(n\), position \(k\) of
Pascal's Triangle.

\begin{center}\rule{0.5\linewidth}{0.5pt}\end{center}

\textbf{31.} Expand \((H + T)^3\) where \(H\) and \(T\) represent heads
and tails.

This represents all possible outcomes of 3 coin flips!

\textbf{32.} From your expansion, identify: - (a) How many ways to get 0
heads (all tails)? - (b) How many ways to get exactly 1 head? - (c) How
many ways to get exactly 2 heads? - (d) How many ways to get 3 heads? -
(e) Total number of outcomes?

\textbf{33.} For a fair coin (\(p = 0.5\)), expand \((0.5 + 0.5)^3\) to

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The Compound Interest Formula:}

\[A = P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}\]

Where: - \(A\) = final amount - \(P\) = principal (initial amount) -
\(r\) = annual interest rate (as decimal) - \(n\) = number of times
compounded per year - \(t\) = time in years
\end{quote}
