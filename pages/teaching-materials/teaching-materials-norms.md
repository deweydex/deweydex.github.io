Norms & Expectations

Introduction — Learning Happens in Your Head

Let me start by saying what this text is not: this is not a list of rules. Ours is a classroom where we each, as individuals, learn various things together. Most of the learning will not happen in this classroom, but rather on your own time, whether at home, your favorite coffee shop, pub, or library. Studying can certainly happen in groups, and group assignments will almost certainly arise over the course of our time together, but the learning can only happen in your head. You can only demonstrate that learning through effort spent in working on assignments, assessments, projects, reflections, and other products. Can some of those sessions be done in groups—absolutely! I just want to emphasize that the actual learning, and the evidence of that learning in what you produce, happens by connecting exactly one brain to the keyboard. With that out of the way, let’s jump right into some administrivia!

Short Version: Mutual Respect + Communication + Self-Honesty

You are welcome to read the words below, or refer to the FAQs section (which may or may not be under construction) but the general way that I conduct my classes is less about rules and more about norms and equitable treatment. Ours is a group made up of (young) adults, meaning that everyone is in my class because they seek to achieve a certain goal. What that goal is likely depends (at least somewhat) on the individual, though it is likely partially dicated by the educational and economic structure in which we find ourselves.

I genuinely want you, and every student in our class, to succeed. That success needs to take into account where each learner starts and how far they have progressed. With this in mind, my assessments tend to either use online resources or are project based. Both require you to demonstrate mastery of certain competencies, but both do not involve terminal grades after one attempt, but rather a variety of opportunities for improvement. As in all things, the more effort you put in, the more you engage with the material and methods in this course, and the more you demonstrate that effort and engagement through participation, submission of assignments, and reflections, the more easily I can see what you have learned, the more easily I can intuit where you might need some extra assistance or resources, and the more time and flexibility I will be willing to give you.

As this is a classroom of adults, it is my assumption that you are able to either articulate or simply act upon your needs: You do not need to ask permission to use the washroom, drink water, eat food, take a call (outside of the classroom), take a five minute break or request one for the group, etc. Since we will often be surrounded by computers, this involves some common sense changes to behavior (open beverages are to be handled with caution, oily food near keyboards doesn’t respect that device’s next user, and so on). Overall, this is a space where we are all here to learn, that means accomodating each other’s learning styles, educational background, propensities and difficulties, successes and struggles. 

Communication is a two way street, I will do my best to be both clear and concise (that last one is far harder for me) in my communications with you, so that you can know what to expect, what changes arise, and what to look out for. For more about communication see below!

Attendence and Punctuality

It is assumed that you attend every class, from start to finish and attendence will be taken. This is especially important for those who recieve welfare or are part of certain governmental program. For the first portion of nearly every class we will engage in intention setting and planning, for the last portion of nearly every class we will engage in reflection, if you are late, or must leave early, please let me know the reason via email or message and submit your planning or reflection as usual within 48 hours. If you know that you will be late or must leave early, please send me a message with a brief explanation as soon as you are aware of the delay. Should lateness or leaving early prove to be a pattern, please expect me to reach out so that we can have a conversation: the goal is to aid in your learning, not to punish or chastize you. If you are struggling generally to meet other expectations of the course, let us figure out some ways that your learning can best be supported. 

Illness and Other Absences

If there are any unforeseen absences, for example you are sick or had to handle a personal emergency just go ahead and let me know via email/message. If a pattern emerges or you will be out for a sigificant amount of time (or have some kind of scheduling conflict), the general advice is the same: just let me know, let’s talk about it and we will work something out.

Communication and Professionalism

In general we will use (Moodle?) as well as email for communication about our work together. Handouts will appear on Moodle but can also be found here (and should be sent to your email once settings have been so configured). The following are some general ideas about communication as well as specific norms that will help us to communicate better, both between teacher and student and amongst yourselves as students.

How we communicate in a given context depends on lots of factors big and small: everything from our age, mother-tongue, dialect, upbringing, culture and prior educational and professional experiences shape how we speak and write to each other. Today, sometimes it can be difficult to understand how to address people in different circumstances. During my courses I try to include a day where we discuss communication, but I can give a brief overview of expectations here. The general idea is to think of all communications we share as taking place within a professional context.

1. When we write to each other (and when we speak to each other), no matter what medium (email, chat, code comment, etc.) we should use full sentences whenever possible and avoid using words that refer to other words or ideas like “that, it, thing” or even “the assignment” or “the question” when the other person might not super clear what particular “thing” is being referenced. 
2. In written communication we will use capitalization (especially of words like I and proper nouns) and be as grammatical as possible (sometimes, when discussing math and computer science terms, grammar can be confusing!). If you need any advice or help with your writing, there are lots of great tools online (Grammarly for example) and there are plenty of folks who can help around, just ask!
3. We want to explain answers to problems and questions, no matter the subject; this means more than showing your work, it also means “teaching to the page” which we will discuss more in class, and in an upcoming handout.

Notes and Note Taking

In many classes there are those who worry that if they do not take notes they will miss a key piece of information or not know how to study properly. In my class you can expect me to be fully transparent about my policies, assignments, assessments, and projects as well as why and how I made the decisions that shaped them. One of my main goals is that you, as a student, become more aware of both how you learn and how to find and evaluate the information you need. Sometimes this means abandoning old habits, or creating new ones: while note-taking is certainly allowed in my class, you should know that it is not necessary. The information in my courses exist “out there,” none of it is new, none of it is particularly difficult to find. I will of course provide recommendations and resources, as well as handouts and assignments that I have created to supplement and support your learning. But “missing something” is not a possibility in my courses: if you learn the material of the module (which will be posted publically and repeatedly), how you learned it is less important than the fact that you learned it. There are so many ways of getting excited about and engaging with the stuff we are learning together, I will do my best to provide you with multiple resources with differring looks, feels, and media. All this is to say, notes are welcome, but not necessary. While it can be difficult, try to remember that we are in class to learn stuff and have fun doing so!

End of Year Reflections

Hello fellow learners,



what a year it has been. For those of you expecting a standard feedback form, prepare to be surprised. As our school year comes to a close, there are few questions we might reflect on, these are emotional questions, not academic ones, but don’t let that e-word fool you: they are as much about understanding your subject as they are about understanding your learning. 

Format: this is a webpage, so this isn’t really a form, per-se, the idea is to open up a document (whether word or .txt or a jupyter notebook if you, like me, have become addicted to markdown), and allow your answer to one question flow into the next. This means, unlike normal forms, the idea is that you read all the questions beforehand, and then choose those that most resonate with you. If you would like you can share the answer with me, if not, you are welcome to keep your answer to yourself. Obviously, I cannot force you to reflect, and there are so many pressing things that call for your attention these days, but I hope, given the level of trust that we have built over the past year as part of this class, that you will indulge me this more humanistic exercise. So, without further ado, take a breath, stretch, give yourself a moment to settle, and lets get reflecting.

1. Can you think of a particular topic or idea that you really struggled with before this year that now you feel you understand deeply? What brought about that transition?
2. What specific aspects of our class did you find helpful and enjoyable, what were the parts where you you changed your feelings or opinions? (For example, first you liked something but ended up find it less useful, or the reverse!)
3. What about the culture of the class worked for you or didn’t work for you? Is there some aspect that initially you were frustrated by but eventually you liked (or at least appreciated)? What parts of the cadence of the class worked, and what didn’t? How come?
4. How did you find the assignments and class time? Were you able to connect with me and with colleagues or did you prefer to work on your own? How did your impressions of the material covered in those assignments/classes change overtime?
5. How have your personal goals changed over the course of the year? Did something in our class change what you think and feel about your future? 
6. What is a habit or pattern of behavior that you noticed this year that you would like to hold on to for next year (whatever that year might hold)? Is there a habit that you would like to abandon next year, or perhaps transform into something else? 
7. How would you say your impression of our class’s subject has changed over the course of the year? What was surprising? What is something that if you told your past self, they wouldn’t believe it (or wouldn’t understand it!)?
8. When you think about your level of confidence in our subject, both overall and in class, what are the things that you would say increase that feeling of confidence? What gets in the way? Has this changed over the course of the year? If so, in what way? If not, is there some direction you would like to take to change your confidence level?
9. When studying a subject it can be easy to lose the big picture the “why am I doing this again?” feeling can be really strong: for your future self, what is something that you gained from this class that has changed the way you think about your field overall? Was there a time when you lost your perspective this year? What happened and what did you do to try to get yourself back on track? (Or, if you are in the middle of figuring that out, maybe think through some things you might do to refocus yourself and better understand your goals.)
10. Technical subjects can often feel separate from more creative subjects, but let’s try to abandon that bias! Can you think of a time this year that you felt really creative in our class? Can you think of a time where you were challenged and had to really think differently from the way you thought before in order to move forward? Were there moments where you started doing something a different way and that opened up your understanding?
11. How would you say your emotional state has changed over the course of the year (maybe think of a graph, are there lots of little ups and downs, some big swings up/down, is the curve smooth, or choppy...) ? What things outside of your education had a positive or negative effect on your studies?
Independent Learning Plan

During most classes with me, each of us will generally be learning at our own pace, punctuated by group practice or discussion. In order for that to work, and for all of us to better learn how to set goals and take responsibility of our own learning, occasionally we will each create an Independent Learning Plan. This can be done using a template (check your course page for a link if I have mentioned one!) or just answering the following questions on a physical or digital piece of paper.

Key questions:

1. What will you accomplish over what period of time? It can help to think of a STAR Goal framework: 

* SPECIFIC – Define what you want to achieve. This usually is in the form of a certain number of exercises on a specific topic (For example, 20 problems about factoring polynomials, or 8 exercises involving python for-loops.)
* TIMELY – When do you want to achieve the goal? (A specific date/class-period, try to give yourself enough time to get things done, but not too much time that it feels abstract and not urgent.)
* ACTION ORIENTED – How will you achieve the goal? (What specific steps will you take!)
* REALISTIC – Is the goal attainable? What might stand in your way?

If you need to change the date that you estimated, or run into other issues, that is totally fine, these are guidelines and goals, not written in stone.

If you would like problems or questions from other resources or from me (I always am up for giving nice little problems) just let me know! :) 

2. How you will accomplish your goal? This is usually about discussing what resources you will use as well as what amount of time you will spend. Since part of your goal might involve producing something (like a computer program that generates random numbers) you can outline the steps you would take to get that done.  What specific actions will you take? Where will you find your exercises? What file structure will you use for your project? Is there a framework or step-by-step process you will use? Are there things you will check your program for? Will you make sure you wrote out steps as evidence?

3. What challenges or hurdles might stand in your way? What might make accomplishing your goal difficult? Most importantly: what will you do to overcome those challenges? 

Sometimes the biggest challenges we face are physical, feeling tired, or unfocused. Sometimes it is not clear what the next steps should be or we might be struggling with our confidence level. This section is not about being overly optimistic or pessimistic, but for outlining the real and present issues we might face when we go about a given task.

You are welcome (encouraged even!) to send me your plans (or show me in person) and discuss them with me in class. If you are looking for resources or confused about how to take the next step, or even just unsure about what a useful and realistic goal is, I am here, and feel free to ask a friend about their plan or include them in it as a source of feedback or to check your knowledge.

As always, questions and comments and suggestions are welcome! If some part of the above isn't clear or doesn't make sense, maybe I can fix it to make it better or easier! Good luck!
